Coordinated disclosure & bounty

Flagship is a security-first product. We pay for serious vulnerabilities in a defined scope and respond to reports under a published SLA. Coordinated disclosure is required — exploit details stay private until a fix ships.

Scope

In scope

  • flagshipserver.com — the Cloudflare Worker, all /api/* routes, the static site.
  • webapp.flagshipserver.com — the webapp PWA: WebAuthn / IRK signing / lease deposit / recovery upload paths.
  • remote.flagshipserver.com — the browser-remote surface: the phone-approval ceremony and the keyless session it issues.
  • *.flagship.services — the SNI passthrough plane on Fly, the tunnel hub.
  • The Flagship daemon as shipped by the Flagship builder on the Debian-based boot image. Includes ACME, the local TLS server, the data layer (Postgres / MinIO / Redis / Forgejo / Chromium under /var/flagship/data/).
  • The canonical-bytes layer in @flagship/protocol — sign/verify routines, key derivations, encryption.
  • iOS and Android apps once shipped to TestFlight / Play Console.

Out of scope

  • Vulnerabilities in apps installed by individual users (those are the app author's responsibility; report to them directly).
  • Self-hosted forks of Flagship (only the canonical builds are in scope).
  • Issues requiring physical access to a user's already-unlocked phone or laptop.
  • Theoretical attacks against well-vetted primitives (Ed25519, X25519, AES-128-GCM, SHA-256, HKDF) without a working PoC.
  • Rate-limiting, denial-of-service, or volumetric attacks against public endpoints. (We rely on Cloudflare's edge for these.)
  • Missing security headers on static pages where there's no XSS sink.
  • Reports auto-generated by scanners with no manual analysis.

Bounty payouts

Status: pre-release, not yet fully funded. Flagship has not launched commercially and the bounty programme is not funded yet, so payouts are delayed until it is. The bands below are what we expect to pay once funding is in place — they are not a current offer. Until then we triage, fix, and credit reports under this policy, and we record every qualifying report as an outstanding obligation to be assessed against these bands at commercial launch. We would rather tell you this before you spend your time than after.

What that means in practice, until the programme is funded:

  • We still triage and fix on the published SLA. Funding has no bearing on how fast a report gets dealt with. Both reports we have received so far were fixed and deployed the same day.
  • Every qualifying report is recorded as an outstanding obligation, with the severity we assess it at, to be settled against the bands above at commercial launch.
  • We assess severity ourselves and will rate upwards. If your own write-up is more conservative than the facts support — as the first report we received was — we record the higher severity, not the one you claimed.
  • Credit is available immediately, in whatever form you ask for, and is not contingent on anything. It is the one thing we can give you now, so we would rather give it properly.
  • No embargo once the fix has shipped. Write your findings up publicly whenever you like. Ask us first if you want a technical-accuracy review; we will not ask you to soften anything.
  • If the delay causes you real hardship, tell us. We would rather hear it than have you discover our funding position afterwards and feel misled.

SLA

  • Initial acknowledgement: ≤ 2 business days
  • Triage decision (in/out of scope, severity): ≤ 7 days
  • Fix ship target by severity:
    • Critical: ≤ 7 days
    • High: ≤ 30 days
    • Medium: ≤ 90 days
    • Low: best-effort, usually rolled into the next release.
  • Public disclosure (reporter credit): coordinated with you, after the fix has shipped to all auto-update channels.

How to report

File via the vulnerability report form (preferred — it nudges the right structured fields), or email security@flagshipserver.com. Encrypt sensitive details with our PGP key (download from /security.html; fingerprint posted there and on /.well-known/security.txt).

Please include:

  • A clear description of the issue.
  • The exact endpoint / file / commit hash affected.
  • A working PoC (script, curl invocation, or screenshots).
  • Your assessed impact and severity.
  • Whether you'd like to be credited publicly (and under what name).

Safe-harbor

Good-faith research conducted under this policy will not be subject to legal action by Flagship. You may probe production endpoints provided you don't:

  • Access, modify, or delete other users' data beyond the minimum needed to demonstrate the issue.
  • Use the issue to extract more than test-account data.
  • Run automated scanners that generate sustained load.
  • Publish details before the fix has shipped, or before our mutually agreed disclosure date.

Unauthenticated identity-key swap on an account with no second factor

Critical · reported and fixed October 2026 · fix 7d49a444 · credit pending the reporter’s preference

Account recovery schedules a replacement of the account’s root identity key. The endpoint that starts it verified the request against the incoming key — a self-assertion — and its only other input, the account’s current identity key, is served to anyone by the public username lookup. On an account with no second factor enrolled, that left nothing a stranger could not supply: knowing a public handle was enough to schedule a hostile key swap, which completed once the grace window elapsed.

There was also no owner veto, contrary to what our own documentation claimed. The objection endpoint is signed by the incoming key, so it is the recoverer’s own undo rather than something the displaced owner can exercise. The grace window was a notification window, not a brake.

Starting a recovery now requires an enrolled credential: a TOTP or recovery code, the short-lived proof minted by the passphrase-gated wrapped-key fetch, or a signature from the account’s currently registered key. An account with no credential cannot be recovered by anyone, which is what our naming and recovery policy always said. Finalising a recovery now requires a signature from the key being installed, where it had been an unauthenticated POST. The same pass closed a related fault we had not been told about: with its key unavailable the server fell back to a structural check on TOTP codes, so the second factor was a formality on exactly the accounts that had enrolled one.

Unauthenticated CA-signing oracle mints a relay-hub blessing for any key or host

Critical · reported and fixed October 2026 · fix 54889a4c, hardening 484ef162 and 771db538 · credit pending the reporter’s preference

The endpoint that issues relay-hub blessings did not authenticate its caller, so anyone could obtain a validly signed blessing for a key and host of their choosing — a signing oracle against the trust chain a box uses to decide whether the hub in front of it is the real one.

The issuer is now authenticated and blessings are bound to the expected apex. Two follow-ups harden the box side rather than the hole itself: a box fails closed when the hub presents no blessing and requires the expected apex, and trust lockdown now closes connections that are already open instead of only refusing new ones.
