Setup & install

Plug in any old PC. Boot from one USB stick. Pair with your phone. Most people are online in about ten minutes.

What hardware do I actually need?

Any x86_64 machine with at least 2 GB of RAM, 32 GB of disk, and a working ethernet port. An old laptop, a NUC, an Optiplex from 2014. All fine. (You can also host a server on a computer you already use, from Flagship Studio.) The builder handles the OS for you, so you don't need to know or care what it runs.

How long does install take?

About ten minutes from inserting the USB stick to a real green padlock at you.flagship.services. Most of that is the kernel booting and Let's Encrypt issuing your cert. To make the stick, pair the app directly with Flagship Studio; your phone sends the signed recipe and Studio does the rest.

Will it wipe the disk?

Yes. The installer formats the target disk, sets up LUKS full-disk encryption with a key only your phone can release, and lays down a hardened Linux base. Anything that was on the disk before is gone. The installer asks twice and shows you the device path; we try hard not to surprise anyone.

Do I need a static IP or port forwarding at home?

No. Your box opens an outbound WebSocket to flagship.services; that's the tunnel. No port-forwarding, no dynamic DNS, no router config. CGNAT is fine. The only requirement is that outbound TCP/443 works, which it does on every consumer ISP.

Can I install without a phone?

Not really, but you have options. The phone is the trust root in the strong-security path; there's no email-and-password to fall back to. If you don't want to use a phone, the installable web app at webapp.flagshipserver.com works as a peer device (passphrase + WebAuthn instead of Secure Enclave). It's a weaker key-storage trade-off but it's an account you fully control.

How does Studio pairing work?

Open Flagship Studio on your computer. It shows a QR and a short code. In the mobile app, choose Add server → Pair with Studio, scan or enter the code, and compare the security code on both screens. Your phone signs the recipe and sends it directly to Studio; the pairing session is short-lived and single-use. The webapp offers a signed recipe download instead.

Can I run it on a Raspberry Pi?

Not yet. Today the builder targets x86_64 machines; we don't ship an ARM build of the base OS as a default. The daemon itself is plain TypeScript, so a Pi 4 with an SSD is technically capable; if you get there by hand, please tell us how it went.

Privacy & security

The structural answer to "can you read my data?" is no: the bytes never leave your box decrypted. Here's what that means in practice.

Can Flagship read my data?

Not the content of your data: files, messages, app data and AI prompts never reach us in a readable form. TLS ends on your own box, against a Let's Encrypt certificate whose private key only your box holds, so flagship.services sees encrypted bytes go past on a TCP pipe. What we can see is metadata: that your account exists, which servers it has and when they are online, roughly how much traffic they carry, and the other items listed under "What does Flagship the company actually store?" below. Because we run the flagship.services DNS zone we could also obtain a certificate for a box's name; the apps pin your box's own signed certificate and would refuse it, and any such certificate would be visible in public Certificate Transparency logs, but a plain web browser would not notice. The full list of who sees what is in our metadata page.

What does Flagship the company actually store?

Your username and your devices' public keys; each server's name, public keys, the address its heartbeat arrives from, last-seen times, health reports and the orders sent to it (updates, backup policy, moves); a sealed LUKS-unlock blob (encrypted to your phone); push-notification tokens; the audit events your devices post; and per-account counts of traffic and AI usage. We also keep one list of the outside AI hosts boxes have reached, with totals and no account or box. No file content, no prompts, no chats, no service traffic. The full inventory lives in /privacy.

What if my phone is compromised?

Game over for that account, the same way it's game over for your bank app. The phone is the trust root; a compromised root can sign whatever it wants. That's why the keys are wrapped by a key held in the phone's secure hardware (the Secure Enclave on iPhone, the hardware-backed Keystore on Android), and sensitive actions such as approving a server's start or signing as an admin ask for your biometric. While the app is unlocked, some keys are held in the phone's memory, so a phone that is already hacked and unlocked is not protected. If you suspect a compromise, use any other paired device to revoke the phone's identity key.

What encryption do you use?

Standard primitives only: Ed25519 signatures, X25519 + AES-256-GCM for sealed blobs, HKDF-SHA-256 for key derivation, LUKS2/argon2id for the disk. Real Let's Encrypt certificates, requested by your box itself, for transport. If we ever invent our own crypto, please report it as a bug.

Is the source open?

Yes. The protocol, daemon, image-builder, and clients are at github.com/ibisllc/flagship under BUSL-1.1, which converts to Apache 2.0 in 2030. Reproducible builds for the ISO are on the roadmap so you can verify the bytes match the source.

Can a court compel you to hand over my data?

They can compel us to hand over what we have, and what we have is account and server metadata (see above), a sealed blob, and routing records. We can't decrypt your traffic because we don't terminate it. We can revoke the routing entry for your subdomain (which takes your services offline); that's a power we have and we'd resist abusing. We'll publish a transparency report annually once we have any requests to report.

What do you log?

Cloudflare gets normal request logs (IP, path, status) for flagshipserver.com on a 30-day window; we don't extend them. Our relay on flagship.services writes no per-connection log: it keeps counters in memory (totals every 15 minutes) and shows an error line only when a box's connection is parked too long. Its hosting platform (Fly) may keep connection metadata under its own policy. Your daemon logs locally on your box and never ships those logs anywhere. We don't run analytics, ad pixels, or session-replay on the marketing site or the dashboard.

Services

Services are containers running on your box, namespaced by user.

What is "vibe-coding" a service?

You describe what you want in plain language ("a tracker for which houseplants I've watered, push me when one's been thirsty 5+ days"). An LLM writes the code. Your daemon builds it, picks the data-layer scopes (Postgres tables, object storage, push), and runs it at plant-water.you.flagship.services. About 90 seconds. You can edit the code if you feel like it.

Can I share a service with a friend?

Yes, two ways. Invite people to use one app with a link you create on your phone; each person joins with their own key and you set their rank. Or share the whole server with another account, to view it or to co-manage it (a co-manager can never destroy it). There is no central database: the lists live on your servers, and changes are signed by your admin device.

What stops a malicious service from stealing my data?

The data-layer is per-service and per-user-namespaced: each app gets its own Postgres database and its own files folder, runs in a sandbox (gVisor), and can reach the internet only at hosts you approve. A service for "plant watering" can't read your photos. Nothing stops a malicious service from being terrible inside its own scope, which is why you should think before installing one.

Which AI providers can services use?

Whichever you've configured: bring your own key for Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, OpenRouter or any OpenAI-compatible API. Keys live on your daemon, encrypted at rest. By default your box reaches the provider through our relay address, which carries the encrypted connection and sees the provider's hostname and the amount of data, not the prompts; you can turn that off per key. With no key, building an app uses Flagship Inference, our built-in AI: it passes requests to a third-party model provider without keeping them (see /privacy).

Can I uninstall a service cleanly?

Yes. Uninstall drops its container, its Postgres database, its files folder and its access list. You can keep a snapshot before uninstall if you want to come back to the data later.

If Flagship goes away

A fair question. The product is designed so this is survivable.

What happens to my server if you shut down?

It keeps running, and so do your services, until its certificate runs out (certificates last about 90 days). Several things need us: routing through flagship.services (the tunnel hub is ours), renewing the certificate (our DNS zone is used for the challenge), setting up a new server, pairing a new device, account recovery and any routing change. The code is open, so someone could run their own copies, but we have not built a handover for that, and we don't promise one.

What about my data and identity?

The data is on your disk; it was never anywhere else. The identity is your phone's keypair; it doesn't depend on us being alive. The username at flagshipserver.com goes away if the company does, but the underlying public key is yours. Keep a backup of your account key and of your server (Back up now) so you can rebuild elsewhere.

Will the source survive you?

Yes. BUSL-1.1 with a Change Date of 2030-05-03: even if the company evaporates tomorrow, the entire codebase becomes Apache 2.0 in 2030 by license, and the GitHub mirror stays up regardless.

Didn't find it?

Mail support@flagshipserver.com. A real person reads everything. For broken installs, try /help first; for security questions, /security goes deeper than this page does.
